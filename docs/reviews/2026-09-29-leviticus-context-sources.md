# Leviticus context: source trail and review limits

Prepared 2026-09-29 for the [Leviticus book briefing](../../public/scriptures/books/LEV.json). This is an agent review of the hosted text, cited sources, and editorial boundaries. It records **no human or specialist approval**. Chapter authors must ground each guide in its own verses and neighbors; this book-level audit does not certify individual guide prose.

## Hosted text and editions

I read all 27 chapters of the hosted [World English Bible Leviticus](../../public/scriptures/text/web/LEV.json), identified by the site's [Scripture source notes](../../public/scriptures/SOURCES.md). It has **859 verse slots** and **23,181 English words** when each verse string is split on whitespace and the resulting tokens are counted. The reader briefing rounds that to about 23,200 words. These are measurements of this hosted English file, not claims about an ancient Hebrew text or another English edition.

The [Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible source notes](../../public/scriptures/original/SOURCES.md) identify the hosted, word-level Westminster Leningrad Codex layer. I checked the displayed Hebrew forms at their cited verses. That pointed medieval manuscript is useful for locating words; it is not an author's copy or a date for Leviticus's formation. Innocent Himbaza's published survey in [*The Text of Leviticus*](https://www.peeters-leuven.be/pdf/9789042943452.pdf), introduction pp. 2–5, lists the 4Q17 Exodus–Leviticus fragment preserving Leviticus 1:13–15, 1:17, and 2:1 at approximately **250 BCE**. The [Library of Congress exhibit caption](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/scrolls/libr.html) describes a different, fragmentary Cave 11 Leviticus scroll (11Q1) in paleo-Hebrew script and labels its copy late second or early first century BCE. Himbaza's table reports different proposed dates for 11Q1; therefore the LOC date is attributed to that exhibit rather than stated as certain. The LOC caption identifies portions of chapters 4, 10–11, 13–14, 16, and 18–27. All such dates concern surviving copies, not the book's composition.

The hosted [Brenton English Septuagint Leviticus](../../public/scriptures/text/brenton/LEV.json) also has 27 chapters and 859 verse slots, but chapter boundaries are not interchangeable. WEB chapter 5 has 19 slots and chapter 6 has 30; Brenton chapter 5 has 26 and chapter 6 has 23. WEB 6:1–7 corresponds in content to Brenton 5:20–26. This is a comparison of two hosted **English editions**. It does not determine whether an ancient Greek or Hebrew reading is earlier. The [International Organization for Septuagint and Cognate Studies](https://septuaginta.uni-goettingen.de/ioscs/editions/) lists a critical Greek Leviticus edition assembled from multiple witnesses; Brenton's English text is not that edition.

## Source access and unresolved review

The USCCB introduction and cited chapter notes, Yale's two-page Part 1 guide and accessible Part 2 guide, the Angelini, Collins, and Glatt-Gilad articles, the full Himbaza introduction in Peeters's publisher PDF, the Library of Congress caption, and the hosted files were checked directly. Cambridge Scarlata pages supplied **publisher abstracts**, not full chapters; their source descriptions were narrowed. Watts supplied a **publisher summary**, not the full chapter. The Janowitz article text was accessible. The inherited Ramban introduction link displayed a Sefaria shell without the commentary; its unsupported summary and source entry were removed. The Cambridge Greek-book URL was corrected to the accessible publisher page. The source catalog's local hosted entries now use resolvable `href` paths to public notes.

The most important open specialist questions are the range of each Hebrew ritual term, dating and sequence of Priestly/Holiness composition, the original referent of Azazel, the textual history behind the WEB/Brenton chapter 5–6 boundary, and how later Jewish traditions interpret these laws in practice. The named scholarly proposals are positions, not a count of consensus. No modern medical diagnosis, settled date of Sinai events, or application of ancient penalties was inferred.
