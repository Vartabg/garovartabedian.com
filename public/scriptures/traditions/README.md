# Scripture tradition briefings

These files explain received canons as historical and ecclesial reading
practices, not as a contest over who has the largest book count. Every
tradition route should answer five different questions:

1. Which church family or present-day edition does the route represent?
2. How did its received collection and textual base take shape?
3. Which contents or arrangements distinguish it from the other shelves?
4. How does the community actually receive, interpret, and use Scripture?
5. What can this site host, and what remains absent or only approximately
   represented by public-domain English texts?

## Evidence standard

Every fact, formation moment, distinctive, reception claim, variation, debate
position, and hosted-text boundary carries `sourceIds`. Each id must resolve to
a stable entry in `sources`. Prefer current church documents for what a church
teaches about itself and use academic reception history to test claims that a
single official list fully describes a complex manuscript tradition.

Do not turn one local edition into a universal rule. In particular:

- “Protestant” is a family of confessional and liturgical traditions.
- Eastern Orthodox Greek, Slavonic, and other local editions have differences
  at the edges and in their appendices.
- The Ethiopian formula “81 books” uses multiple grouping conventions and
  cannot be reconstructed from this site’s English overlap shelf.
- Historical Armenian manuscript extras do not automatically become a
  twenty-eighth book in the current Armenian Apostolic New Testament.

## Hosted shelf rule

The route’s `Tradition.books` array controls what can be opened in the reader;
the briefing’s `hosting` section controls what the page may claim about that
array. Never describe an overlap shelf as a complete ecclesial Bible when the
source language, distinctive books, or current approved edition is missing.
